Research Interests

We explore the darkest corners of the Universe — from black holes lurking at the hearts of galaxies to the dark matter halos enveloping galaxies and galaxy clusters — with the ultimate goal of understanding the role of central black holes in the formation and evolution of their host systems.

Black Holes Physics
We investigate black holes across the full mass spectrum and cosmic time, to understand how they form, grow, and co-evolve with their host galaxies.

Galaxy Dynamics & Evolution
We study how galaxies and their central black holes co-evolve across cosmic time, tracing changes in their structural and kinematic properties through photometry, stellar kinematics, and dynamical modelings.

Dark Matter
We probe its distribution and properties through gravitational signatures such as stellar dynamics and gravitational lensing.

Stellar Populations & Initial Mass Function (IMF)

Hubble Space Telescope
Low Earth Orbit
Launched in 1990, HST has provided unprecedented views of the universe across a wide range of wavelengths. We use HST data extensively to model galaxies' photometric properties and morphology, and to conduct inspection of their visual features (such as dust lanes, star clusters, and jets).

James Webb Space Telescope
Sun-Earth L2
Launched in 2021, JWST carries NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS, enabling high-resolution infrared spectroscopy of faint, distant objects. We primarily use NIRSpec integral-field data to measure stellar kinematics inside compact galaxy nuclei, placing tight constraints on BH parameters.

Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array
Atacama Desert, Chile
66 high-precision antennas that capture the emission of cold molecular gas and dust. We use ALMA to trace the rotation of gas under a black hole's gravitational influence, allowing us to indirectly measure the central dark mass without ever "seeing" the BH directly.

W. M. Keck Observatory
Mauna Kea, Hawai'i
Two 10-meter telescopes atop Mauna Kea equipped with advanced adaptive optics and instruments including OSIRIS and KCWI. We use Keck to zoom deep into the bright, dense nuclear star clusters of nearby galaxies and to derive stellar population and dark matter properties.

Four 8.2-meter telescopes atop Cerro Paranal equipped with advanced adaptive optics and instruments including SINFONI and MUSE. We use VLT to study the kinematics of stars in the vicinity of central black holes.

Black Holes Physics

Origins & growths of black hole seeds

Image: Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration

The origin and growth of supermassive black holes is one of the most hotly debated questions in modern astrophysics. Theories predict that the first black hole seeds formed within the earliest 200 million years after the Big Bang — either as the stellar remnants of the first generation of massive stars (Population III) or through the direct collapse of massive gas clouds — yet JWST has revealed billion-solar-mass black holes already in place within the first billion years, challenging our understanding of how rapidly they could have grown. In the local Universe, the tight correlations between black hole mass and host galaxy properties point to a profound co-evolutionary history, but how and when this connection was established remains an open question. We tackle this problem by measuring precise dynamical black hole masses across a range of galaxy types and cosmic epochs, building the observational foundation needed to discriminate between competing models of black hole seeding and growth.

We combine imaging and spectroscopy from the world's premier facilities: HST, JWST, ALMA, Keck, VLT, Gemini, Magellan, and the upcoming ELT, to study the environments of galaxies and black holes across the mass spectrum. Using stellar/gas kinematics and dynamics, we directly measure black hole masses ranging from intermediate-mass black holes lurking in nearby dwarf galaxies to ultramassive black holes at the hearts of distant giant elliptical galaxies, mapping the full demographic landscape of black holes in the Universe.

Galaxy Dynamics & Evolution

How Galaxy Structures Grow & Change Over Cosmic History

Image: NASA, ESA, Adam G. Riess (STScI, JHU)

Galaxies are not static — they grow, merge, and transform over billions of years, shaped by the interplay of gravity, gas, star formation, and feedback from their central black holes. Understanding how galaxy structures such as disks, bulges, bars, and spiral arms form and evolve across cosmic time is fundamental to understanding our own cosmic origins.

We use deep imaging and spectroscopy from space- and ground-based observatories to trace how galaxies change their shapes, sizes, and internal properties from the early Universe to the present day.

Dark Matter

Investigating dark matter properties through stellar dynamics & strong gravitational lensing

Image: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI, Christopher Willott (NRC-Canada), Lamiya Mowla (Wellesley College), Kartheik Iyer (Columbia)

Dark matter — the invisible component comprising ~85% of all matter in the Universe — remains one of the most profound mysteries in modern physics. Although it cannot be observed directly, its presence is revealed through gravitational effects such as stellar dynamics and gravitational lensing. We exploit these observational signatures to map the distribution of dark matter around massive galaxies and galaxy clusters, ultimately aiming to constrain its fundamental nature and physical properties.

Stellar Populations & Initial Mass Function (IMF)

Are All Stars Created Equal?

Image: Dr. Francesco D'Eugenio and The LEGA-C Survey

Stars are the fundamental building blocks of galaxies, and the Initial Mass Function (IMF), the distribution of stellar masses at birth, is one of the most consequential yet debated quantities in astrophysics. Whether the IMF is universal or varies with environment, metallicity, and cosmic time has profound implications for our understanding of galaxy formation, chemical enrichment, and the stellar mass-to-light ratios used throughout extragalactic astronomy. Recent evidence from Gaia and large spectroscopic surveys suggests the IMF is not universal but depends on metallicity and star formation conditions, challenging long-standing assumptions embedded in stellar population models.
