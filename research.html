Black Hole Physics

Research on the formation and growth of galactic black holes

Image source: EHT Collaboration.

Black holes (BHs) are one of the most mysterious objects in the universe. Theory showed they form from gravitational collapse of massive stars; observations also showed that galaxies can host supermassive black holes (SMBHs) in their core. How exactly do they evolve and settle in the galaxy nucleus? We use imaging and spectroscopy data from various facilities (such as HST, JWST, ALMA, ELT) to extract information from the environment around extragalactic BHs. We apply analysis techniques such as stellar kinematics and gas dynamics to probe the BH's influence and model its properties. We are also exploring gravitational lensing as a way to extend our reach to even more distant and ancient galaxies.

Our work focuses on measuring SMBHs and hunting for intermediate-mass black holes (IMBHs) — the missing pieces in our understanding of BH growth. Key questions include: What is the origin of BH seeds in the early universe? How do SMBHs evolve from stellar mass? What do IMBHs tell us about the BH evolution chain? How can next-generation telescopes probe the properties of IMBHs?

Galaxy Evolution

Research on the evolution and properties of galactic bodies

Galaxies are large and complex systems composed of stars, dust, and matter. We live in one — the Milky Way — and look beyond it to find an impressive variety of systems with different shapes and sizes. BHs can reside in the compact cores of galaxies, including our own. These galactic BHs exert gravitational influences that consume nearby stars, alter their movements, and sometimes eject gas and matter into the circumgalactic medium through powerful jets. Such feedback can heavily affect the global properties of galaxies.

By probing deep into the galactic core and studying the BH inside, we aim to establish a better understanding of how BHs co-evolve with their host galaxies. We are also interested in how galaxies of different mass scales evolved by analysing their stellar population and the dynamics of their components.

Facilities & Instruments

Main telescopes used in our works

Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array (ALMA)

ALMA is a large radio telescope array situated in Chile. Its 66 antennas collect signals across a broad range of frequencies, capturing the emission of cold gas and dust. Using ALMA data, we extract the movement of molecular gas under a black hole's gravitational influence — allowing us to indirectly "see" the central black hole and derive its properties.

James Webb Space Telescope (JWST)

Launched in 2021 and situated at the Sun–Earth L2 point, JWST carries instruments designed to observe the infrared range — NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS. These enable high-resolution imaging and spectroscopy of faint, distant objects. With JWST we probe stellar kinematics inside compact galactic cores for better constraints on BH parameters.

ESO's Extremely Large Telescope (ELT)

Currently under construction in Chile, the ELT's 39-meter primary mirror and adaptive optics will make it the largest telescope in the world. Our current work uses simulation software to generate mock spectroscopy cubes from the HARMONI and MICADO instruments — assessing ELT's performance and evaluating strategies for finding IMBHs.

Keck Observatory

Two telescopes located atop Mauna Kea, equipped with advanced adaptive optics and instruments like OSIRIS and KCWI. We use Keck to zoom into the bright and dense nuclear star clusters of nearby galaxies and to derive stellar population and dark matter properties.

Giant Magellan Telescope (GMT)

Another next-generation ground-based telescope currently under construction in Chile — part of the future landscape for our observational campaigns.
