Science

Research Interests

We explore the darkest corners of the Universe — from black holes lurking at the hearts of galaxies to the dark matter halos enveloping galaxies and galaxy clusters — with the ultimate goal of understanding the role of central black holes in the formation and evolution of their host systems.

Black Holes Physics
We investigate black holes across the full mass spectrum and cosmic time, to understand how they form, grow, and co-evolve with their host galaxies.
Learn More
Galaxy Dynamics & Evolution
We study how galaxies and their central black holes co-evolve across cosmic time, tracing changes in their structural and kinematic properties through photometry, stellar kinematics, and dynamical modeling.
Learn More
Dark Matter
Dark matter — the invisible component comprising ~85% of all matter in the Universe — remains one of the most profound mysteries in modern physics. Although it cannot be observed directly, its presence is revealed through gravitational effects such as stellar dynamics and gravitational lensing. We exploit these observational signatures to map the distribution of dark matter around massive galaxies and galaxy clusters, ultimately aiming to constrain its fundamental nature and physical properties.

Hubble Space Telescope
Low Earth Orbit
Launched in 1990, HST has provided unprecedented views of the universe across a wide range of wavelengths. We use HST data extensively to model galaxies' photometric properties and morphology, and to conduct inspection of their visual features (such as dust lanes, star clusters, and jets).
Current

Image credit: ESA/Hubble World Bank

James Webb Space Telescope
Sun-Earth L2
Launched in 2021, JWST carries NIRCam, NIRSpec, MIRI, and FGS/NIRISS, enabling high-resolution infrared spectroscopy of faint, distant objects. We primarily use NIRSpec integral-field data to measure stellar kinematics inside compact galactic cores, placing tight constraints on BH parameters.
Current

Image credit: ESO, NAOJ, NRAO

Atacama Large Millimeter/submillimeter Array
Atacama Desert, Chile
66 high-precision antennas that capture the emission of cold molecular gas and dust. We use ALMA to trace the rotation of gas under a black hole's gravitational influence, allowing us to indirectly measure the central dark mass without ever "seeing" the BH directly.
Current

Image credit: NASA-JPL/T. Wynne

W. M. Keck Observatory
Mauna Kea, Hawai'i
Two 10-meter telescopes atop Mauna Kea equipped with advanced adaptive optics and instruments including OSIRIS and KCWI. We use Keck to zoom deep into the bright, dense nuclear star clusters of nearby galaxies and to derive stellar population and dark matter properties.

Very Large Telescope
Cerro Paranal, Chile
Four 8.2-meter telescopes atop Cerro Paranal equipped with advanced adaptive optics and instruments including SINFONI and MUSE. We use VLT to study the kinematics of stars in the vicinity of central black holes.

Currently in commissioning, Rubin will soon begin a 10-year survey of the southern sky. With its wide field of view and deep imaging capabilities, Rubin will be a powerful tool for discovering new dwarf galaxies and hunting for intermediate-mass black holes in the local universe.

Extremely Large Telescope
Cerro Armazones, Chile
Currently under construction in Chile, the ELT's 39-meter primary mirror and laser guide star adaptive optics will make it the largest optical/infrared telescope in the world. We use HARMONI and MICADO simulation software to generate mock spectroscopy cubes, evaluate BH measurement strategies, and forecast IMBH and SMBH science cases.

Las Campanas, Chile
Another next-generation ground-based telescope under construction at Las Campanas Observatory. With seven primary mirror segments and a 25-meter effective aperture, GMT will complement ELT for high-resolution spectroscopic studies of black holes and their host galaxies in the southern sky.

Black Holes

Formation and growth of galactic black holes

Image: Event Horizon Telescope Collaboration

Black holes (BHs) are one of the most mysterious objects in the universe. Theory showed they form from gravitational collapse of massive stars; observations also showed that galaxies can host supermassive black holes (SMBHs) in their core. How exactly do they evolve and settle in the galaxy nucleus?

We use imaging and spectroscopy data from various facilities (HST, JWST, ALMA, ELT) to extract information from the environment around extragalactic BHs. We apply stellar kinematics and gas dynamics to probe the BH's influence and model its properties. Our work focuses on measuring and hunting for intermediate-mass black holes (IMBHs) in nearby dwarf galaxies, and ultramassive black holes in distant giant elliptical galaxies.

Galaxy Evolution

Properties and evolution of galactic bodies

Image: NASA, ESA, Adam G. Riess (STScI, JHU)

Galaxies are large and complex systems composed of stars, dust, and matter. BHs residing in compact galactic cores exert gravitational influences that consume nearby stars, alter their movements, and sometimes eject gas into the circumgalactic medium through powerful jets — heavily affecting global galaxy properties.

By studying the BH inside galactic cores, we aim to understand how BHs co-evolve with their host galaxies. We are also interested in galaxies of different mass scales, analysing their stellar populations and components' dynamics.

Dark Matter

Investigating dark matter properties through gravitational lensing

Image: NASA, ESA, CSA, STScI, Christopher Willott (NRC-Canada), Lamiya Mowla (Wellesley College), Kartheik Iyer (Columbia)

Dark matter is the mysterious component of the universe that does not emit or absorb light but exerts gravitational effects. It is thought to make up about 27% of the universe's mass-energy content, yet its nature remains elusive. Gravitational lensing, the bending of light by massive objects, provides a powerful tool to probe dark matter's distribution and properties around galaxy clusters and black holes.
